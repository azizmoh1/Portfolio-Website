Actuator Cooling Coil

Using CFD and structural analysis to guide coolant selection and support design.

I built a nine-case ANSYS study of a two-turn copper cooling coil for a robotic shoulder actuator. I compared three coolants across three Reynolds numbers, transferred temperature and pressure loads into a structural model, and isolated the source of stress. The study identified a low-flow water candidate and showed how support constraints influenced its structural response.

01 / Engineering approach

Building a traceable CFD-to-FEA workflow.

I used the supplied coil geometry to define a consistent comparison across water, 40% propylene glycol and mineral oil at Re = 10³, 10⁵ and 10⁷. The copper tube has a 9.525 mm outside diameter, a 0.762 mm wall and an approximately 8 mm bore. I modeled both straight leads as fixed supports to evaluate the effect of restrained thermal expansion.

1. Define and verify the flow conditions

I calculated inlet velocity from v = Re·μ/(ρd), checked volume flow against Fluent’s inlet flux report, and monitored pressure, outlet temperature, mass balance and energy balance. The model used a 293 K inlet and a zero-gauge-pressure outlet.

Re = 1,000 used laminar flow; the higher-Re cases used realizable k–ε with standard wall functions.

2. Connect thermal and structural models

The thermal model prescribed an 80 °C outer copper surface and heat loss of 2,150 W/m² at the inner wall. Fluent used the corresponding positive heat flux into the coolant.

I imported copper temperature and Fluent wall pressure into Static Structural, with a 25 °C stress-free reference and 280 MPa copper yield strength.

Thermal modeling scope. A common prescribed heat flux sets heat removal near 36 W, providing a consistent basis for comparing coolant temperature rise and structural loading.

02 / Results and verification

Quantifying performance and identifying the controlling load.

Water at Re = 1,000 warmed from 293 K to 294.38 K. The copper remained at 79.996–80.000 °C under the imposed boundary conditions. Its global von Mises stress reached 232.49 MPa, giving a safety factor of 1.2044.

Isolating the controlling load

I repeated the structural solution with the pressure load suppressed to separate pressure-driven stress from thermal effects. Maximum stress remained at 232.49 MPa at the reported precision, while displacement changed from 0.10399 to 0.10397 mm. This comparison identified restrained thermal expansion as the dominant load in the low-flow water case.

Quantifying mesh sensitivity

I increased the solid sweep from 100 to 120 divisions and compared the resulting peak stress. Global stress changed from 233.43 to 232.49 MPa, a 0.40% decrease. The final solid mesh had 30,783 nodes and 4,680 elements; the CFD mesh had 49,000 cells.

This check quantified sensitivity to axial mesh refinement.

03 / Design judgment

Turning simulation results into design recommendations.

My recommendation

I recommended water at Re = 1,000 for further development because it had the lowest flow rate and coil pressure drop among the nine cases: 0.375 L/min and 96.11 Pa.

Based on the load-isolation study, I proposed evaluating a compliant or guided lead connection that allows thermal expansion while maintaining positional support.

The next design step is to model that connection and repeat the structural analysis. Pump sizing would then account for the full coolant loop and the selected pump’s performance curve.

Modeling scope and further verification

I treated the extreme high-Re cases as exploratory screening results. Their interpretation requires accounting for constant-property assumptions, omitted viscous heating, turbulent wall resolution and the limits of linear-elastic structural analysis.

Further verification would refine the support-region and through-wall meshes, examine stress averaging and evaluate connection compliance. Comparing cooling capacity would require a coolant-dependent thermal model and experimental measurements. The completed work is a simulation study.
