Thermal-fluids + structural analysis

Actuator Cooling Coil

Choosing a coolant is only part of the design problem. The coil also needs room to expand.

I evaluated a two-turn copper coil for a robotic shoulder actuator across three coolants and three Reynolds numbers. By separating pressure and temperature loads, I identified restrained thermal expansion as the controlling load in the low-flow candidate.

01 / Engineering approach

From flow conditions to structural loads.

The supplied geometry uses 9.525 mm OD copper tube, a 0.762 mm wall and an 8 mm nominal bore. Two helical turns surround the actuator; both straight leads are fixed in the structural model. I studied Re = 10³, 10⁵ and 10⁷ for each coolant, covering a broad range of flow conditions.

1. Calculate and solve the flow

I calculated inlet velocity from v = Re·μ/(ρd), checked volume flow against Fluent’s inlet flux report, and monitored pressure, outlet temperature, mass balance and energy balance. The model used a 293 K inlet and a zero-gauge-pressure outlet.

Re = 1,000 used laminar flow; the higher-Re cases used realizable k–ε with standard wall functions.

2. Transfer temperature and pressure

The thermal model prescribed an 80 °C outer copper surface and heat loss of 2,150 W/m² at the inner wall. Fluent used the corresponding positive heat flux into the coolant.

I imported copper temperature and Fluent wall pressure into Static Structural, with a 25 °C stress-free reference and 280 MPa copper yield strength.

Thermal modeling scope. A common prescribed heat flux sets heat removal near 36 W, providing a consistent basis for comparing coolant temperature rise and structural loading.

02 / Results and verification

Connecting flow performance with structural response.

Water at Re = 1,000 warmed from 293 K to 294.38 K. The copper remained at 79.996–80.000 °C under the imposed boundary conditions. Its global von Mises stress reached 232.49 MPa, giving a safety factor of 1.2044.

Isolating the controlling load

Suppressing the pressure load left the maximum stress at 232.49 MPa at the reported precision. Displacement changed from 0.10399 to 0.10397 mm. That comparison identified thermal restraint as the main driver for the low-flow water case.

Checking sensitivity

Increasing the solid sweep from 100 to 120 divisions changed global stress from 233.43 to 232.49 MPa, a 0.40% decrease. The final solid mesh had 30,783 nodes and 4,680 elements; the CFD mesh had 49,000 cells.

This check quantified sensitivity to axial mesh refinement.

Compare all nine operating cases

PG40 is 40% propylene glycol by mass in water. The high-flow cases are exploratory model outputs; stresses beyond the elastic range require nonlinear analysis for physical interpretation.

03 / Design judgment

A low-flow candidate and a path for further development.

My recommendation

I selected water at Re = 1,000 as the starting point for further development because it has the lowest flow and pressure demand.

I would evaluate a compliant or guided lead connection that permits thermal expansion while preventing rigid-body motion, then repeat the structural analysis.

These support changes were proposed, not simulated. Pump selection would also need the losses of the full coolant loop and a real pump curve.

Model limits and the next validation steps

The high-Re cases exceed the useful range of the simplified constant-property liquid model. Viscous heating was off, turbulent wall resolution was not fully adequate, and the most extreme stresses and displacements exceed linear-elastic applicability.

A future study should refine support-region and through-wall meshes, check stress averaging, model connection compliance, and use a coolant-dependent thermal model before comparing heat-transfer performance. No prototype or experimental validation was performed.
