Systems Design Engineering Internship | Wilo USA

Standardized Hydronic Pump Package Development for Daikin AGZ Chillers

During my internship, I contributed to the development, organization, and standardization of a modular hydronic pump package family intended to integrate with Daikin AGZ air-cooled chiller systems. The goal was to support repeatable package designs across multiple chiller capacities while simplifying engineering, sales, manufacturing, quoting, and future product expansion.

Project Type: Commercial HVAC / hydronic pump skid product family development Capacity Range: standardized package family spanning roughly 30-230 tons Role: Systems Design Intern - mechanical design support, BOM/configuration management, costing support, and package standardization support
Core Problem

Turn custom layouts into a repeatable product platform.

Rather than treating each pump skid as a one-off configuration, the project focused on standardizing package architecture across capacities and operating requirements.

Engineering Scope

Pumps, tanks, VFDs, piping, controls, and documentation.

Each package combined mechanical, hydraulic, electrical, structural, and commercial considerations into one skid-mounted solution.

Portfolio Value

Real systems thinking, not just a model screenshot.

This project shows exposure to product development, configuration data, drawing interpretation, and the practical constraints behind packaged mechanical equipment.

Engineering Objectives

What the Package Had to Do

The project was about making future work easier: fewer one-off decisions, clearer documentation, and a scalable path for package variants.

01Develop standardized pump skid configurations for multiple chiller tonnage ranges.
02Create repeatable package designs that could scale across product variants.
03Standardize component selections across the product family.
04Organize package costing information for quoting support.
05Improve consistency across drawings, submittals, data tables, and package definitions.
06Integrate hydronic system equipment into a unified skid-mounted solution.

System Architecture

Major Subsystems Included

A good package layout has to think beyond the pump. It needs hydraulic performance, maintainability, electrical coordination, manufacturability, and clean documentation.

Hydronic PumpsWilo pump selections and configurations across multiple tonnage ranges.
MotorsMotor selections spanning approximately 1 HP to 5 HP with 1150 RPM and 1750 RPM configurations.
VFDsVariable frequency drive selections matched to pump and motor requirements.
TanksExpansion tank and buffer tank families organized by capacity and system need.
Water QualityAir/dirt separation, side-stream filtration, and chemical shot feeder provisions.
Skid FeaturesFrame integration, rigging points, pipe insulation, optional heat tracing, and chiller-frame interface.

Workflow

From Equipment Data to Standardized Packages

My work sat in the practical middle: understanding the mechanical package, organizing the configuration information, and helping the project become easier to repeat.

Step 01

Study the package architecture

Reviewed package layouts, drawing views, and component callouts to understand how the chiller, skid, pumps, tanks, valves, piping, VFDs, and controls fit together.

Step 02

Connect components to system function

Mapped the purpose of each subsystem so the package could be explained as an engineered solution, not just a collection of parts on a base frame.

Step 03

Support configuration organization

Helped structure package information around repeated selections, capacity ranges, VFD string-code data, and the documentation needed for quoting or future variants.

Step 04

Communicate the system clearly

Translated drawings and technical data into a clear project narrative that connects mechanical design work to manufacturability, deployment, and business value.

Personal Engineering Takeaway

My biggest lesson: standardization is a design problem.

Before this project, it would have been easy to think of CAD as the center of the work. The internship showed me that real product development also depends on repeatable data, clean documentation, supplier/component coordination, service access, and package definitions that other teams can actually use.

Lessons Learned

What I Took From the Internship

Mechanical systems need context.

A pump selection only makes sense when connected to the tank, piping, controls, service access, frame, and customer-facing documentation.

Documentation is engineering.

Clear drawings, organized tables, and consistent naming reduce friction for sales, manufacturing, quoting, and future design work.

Product thinking matters.

The strongest engineering solution is often the one that can be repeated, maintained, and expanded without reinventing the system every time.
